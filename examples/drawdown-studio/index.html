Handweaving · draft notation

Drawdown

A weaver does not draw cloth. They write down three things: which shaft each warp thread is threaded through, which shafts each treadle lifts, and the order the treadles are pressed. The cloth is whatever those three produce. Change a square in any of the three grids and the cloth answers.

An end is one warp thread, running away from you down the loom. A pick is one throw of weft, running across. A float is how far a thread travels over its crossings before it is tied down again; long ones catch on things.

Weaving

Reading the four grids

Threading, along the top

One column per end, left to right, and eight rows for the eight shafts. Shaft 1 is the top row. The filled square says which shaft that end is threaded through. A draft that only needs four shafts leaves the lower four rows empty, and you can still click into them.

Tie-up, at the corner

Eight columns for eight treadles, sharing its rows with the threading above and its columns with the treadling below — which is the whole reason it sits in that corner. A filled square cords that treadle to that shaft. Weavers change this least often; retying a loom takes an afternoon underneath it.

Treadling, down the side

One row per pick, read downward in the order they are woven, aligned with the cloth beside it. The filled square says which treadle is pressed. Walk it one, two, three, four and back to one, and a twill tie-up becomes a diagonal.

The drawdown itself

The only grid you cannot click. A square shows warp where the pick’s treadle lifted the shaft that end is threaded through, and weft where it did not. Everything in it — floats, diagonals, diamonds — follows from the other three.

Demonstration document. The interlacement is computed, not drawn: each square of the cloth is the result of testing the pick’s treadle against the end’s shaft, so the drawdown cannot disagree with the draft above it. Plain weave, 2/2 twill, herringbone, goose eye, and log cabin are standard structures from the handweaving repertoire rather than invented patterns. The dye names are the historical sources — indigo, madder, weld, walnut, iron — and the colours are approximations of them, not measured samples.
