Bent Wood, Bent Rules: American Furniture, 1946–1968

March 14 – September 7, 2026 · Curated by Dana Okafor, Curator of Design

By 1946, molded plywood was no longer a wartime material looking for a peacetime use — it was a genuine argument about how furniture should be made. The techniques the U.S. Navy had funded to produce leg splints and glider parts turned out to bend just as well into the seat of a chair, and a generation of American designers spent the next two decades testing how far that argument could go: how thin the wood could be pressed, how few parts a chair could have, how cheaply something could be built without it looking or feeling cheap.

This exhibition looks at that argument from an unusual angle — not through the New York and Los Angeles studios most familiar to design history, but through the Fenwick Chair Company, a mid-sized manufacturer thirty miles from this museum that spent 1948 to 1963 building molded-plywood and bent-steel furniture for regional department stores, school systems, and a few surprising export contracts. Fenwick's in-house designers, most working under company credit rather than their own names, never had the marketing budget of their coastal counterparts. Their chairs sold on price and durability, not on a designer's byline — which makes the surviving pieces a rare, largely unstudied record of how the same postwar materials and manufacturing logic played out for a company built to sell furniture to a school board, not a museum.

The forty-two objects here — chairs, a stacking table system, and a set of drafting stools that outlasted three different high schools' shop classes — are shown with their original production drawings and factory floor photographs where they survive, most for the first time outside the Fenwick family's own archive.
