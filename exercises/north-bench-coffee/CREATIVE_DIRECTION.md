# Creative Direction — North Bench Coffee Roasters, product-detail page

Practical Exercise 5 (E-commerce/product-detail experience), built under the revised process. This is the sharpest version of the asset-honesty tension in this whole calibration project: `ANTI_VIBE_CODING_UI.md` §3's own asset-recognition table rates product imagery "very high" for physical goods, and this is a fictional roastery with no real product to photograph — the hardest category to do honestly of the six required exercises.

## Product Brief

- **What it does**: a single product-detail page for one specific bag of coffee — needs to get a visitor from "considering" to "add to cart" with real variant choices (size, grind).
- **Primary users**: existing customers of a small specialty roastery, plus new visitors from a referral/social link.
- **Primary task**: understand what's actually in the bag (origin, tasting notes, roast specificity) and buy it with the right size/grind for their equipment.
- **Real reference anchor**: Stumptown Coffee Roasters (`design-reference-atlas.json`, LIVE-verified this project — Windsor serif + GT Flexa, a real named seasonal single-origin product with real origin/varietal data visible on real packaging). The load-bearing lesson from that entry, and from the Comparison 4 matched write-up in `PROFESSIONAL_WEB_DESIGN_RESEARCH.md`, is specific: Stumptown's advantage over a comparable AI-generated coffee page was **being a real business with a real current product catalog**, not a layout technique. This exercise cannot manufacture that advantage — it can only be honest about not having it, and lean as hard as possible on the one thing it *can* do honestly: real-sounding, specific origin/process/tasting content, the same category of asset Stumptown's own page actually uses to carry credibility (specific varietal, specific altitude, specific cooperative), independent of photography.

## No Recipe Without Reason

The obvious template: a large lifestyle product photo (bag on a wood table, steam rising from a cup) as the hero, with the buy box beside it — the standard specialty-e-commerce convention, and the one Stumptown itself likely uses. Rejected for the same reason as Fenwick's Direction A and Northfield's default hero: no real photography exists, and faking it (a stock coffee-bag photo, an AI-generated product render standing in for this specific fictional product) is exactly what §3 forbids for a product whose actual visual identity would be lying about a product that doesn't exist. **This is a real, honest limitation of this exercise, stated plainly rather than solved with a workaround**: an e-commerce product page without product photography is not fully representative of how this category actually works — the content and interaction patterns below are honestly tested, the overall page's real-world persuasiveness is not, and that gap should not be minimized in the eventual review.

## Direction — content-led buy box, honest imagery gap named directly in-product

A single, direct layout (not three structural directions — per §5 proportionality, a single well-reasoned product-detail page doesn't need the full exercise machinery when the deciding constraint, the missing photography, already rules out every direction that would otherwise differentiate the strongest candidates): a compact image-placeholder area (small, not hero-scaled — repeating the Fourth Street Barber lesson about not overstating a placeholder's visual weight), a real buy box (price, size selector, grind selector, quantity, add to cart — all genuinely interactive), and specific origin/process/tasting content given the visual prominence the photo would otherwise have claimed.

## Asset Strategy

Product photography: **must-commission, honestly placeholder in the meantime**, small and clearly labeled, not hero-scaled (applying the Fourth Street Barber finding directly). Logo: plain wordmark.

## Typography

**Fraunces** (display, for the product name and section heads) + **Inter** (reused from Ridgeline, reasoned: a buy box's price/quantity/variant UI benefits from the same highly-legible, dense-numeral-friendly face that reasoning already justified there — not reused by default). Fraunces is on this project's own AI-associated-font list (`TYPOGRAPHY_RESEARCH.md` Part 5) — used anyway, deliberately, per the Typography Decision Framework's own rule that no font is banned by appearing on that list: the question is whether it's checked against this product, not whether the name is familiar. Checked here: a specialty food product genuinely benefits from a warm, slightly editorial serif for its name and story sections, the same register Stumptown's own real Windsor typeface serves — the choice is examined against the product, not reached for because it's convenient.
